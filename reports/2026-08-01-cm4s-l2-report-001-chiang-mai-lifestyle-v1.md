Chiang Mai Lifestyle

The participant is a Chiang Mai resident and event emcee who seeks experiences both for personal enjoyment and to explain local culture and tourism to foreign visitors. Temple visits reliably provide the relaxation, prayer, and photography outcomes they want. By contrast, a Facebook-promoted Japanese festival promised Japanese culture and activities but felt primarily like a food festival with contemporary performances, leading the participant to leave within an hour and regard the visit as wasted time. That mismatch reduced their trust in event promotion and made them more selective, with attendance around once a month considered sufficient. They now reject events using AI-generated promotional images, read social-media feedback, and sometimes ask a trusted friend to attend first and report back privately through LINE. Some friends reportedly share the same uncertainty about whether promoted events will match reality. The central decision implication is that the observed need is not broader event discovery alone, but low-effort, credible evidence that an experience is authentic and matches its promotion before someone commits limited time.

What matters

Top pain points

Event promotion can promise culture, lifestyle, and learning while the actual event primarily offers food or generic entertainment.

The Japanese festival's performances felt contemporary rather than representative of the everyday life or authentic culture the participant expected.

A disappointing event costs scarce personal time; the participant concluded they should not have spent time there.

Promotional claims and images on Facebook are not sufficient for the participant to trust that an event will match its description.

AI-generated promotional images are treated as an immediate signal that the real experience will not resemble the advertisement.

Real photographs still may not provide enough confidence, requiring additional verification from another person.

The participant relies on a friend to assume the risk of attending first, creating a slow and socially costly workaround.

Work limits the participant's ability to try desired experiences such as a homestay.

Without first-hand experiences, the participant lacks the tourist perspective they want for telling useful, credible stories in their work.

The participant can answer some visitors' questions after events but cannot answer everything, and the information appears distributed across websites, Facebook groups, physical places, and personal knowledge.

The disappointing Japanese festival occurred about a year before the interview.

Following that experience, the participant began asking friends whether they had already attended uncertain events.

The participant may tell a friend they are busy, ask the friend to go first, and later request a one-to-one report through LINE.

The participant uses friends with similar interests or perspectives as trusted proxies for judging whether an event is worth attending.

The participant does not attend many events, describes themselves as selective, and considers approximately one event per month acceptable.

The participant first filters events by personal interest, then assesses whether the promotion is trustworthy.

They will not attend when the promotional post or image appears AI-generated.

When promotions contain real images but remain uncertain, they may ask a friend to verify the experience first.

The participant said some friends have the same problem of looking at event promotions and remaining unsure.

Although personally introverted, the participant's emcee work requires speaking with tourists and presenting information in English.

Opportunity hypothesis

Where the evidence points

A bounded hypothesis is that selective, time-constrained event seekers may value a lightweight credibility layer showing recent, real attendee evidence and a clear comparison between advertised and actual experiences. This could reduce dependence on sending trusted friends first while helping the participant identify culturally substantive events. The interview supports the behavior and workaround, but does not establish demand for a new product or willingness to contribute, trust, or pay.
